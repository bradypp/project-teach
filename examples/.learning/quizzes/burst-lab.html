Follow the backlog through a burst

A simplified inbox starts with 20 pending events. For 10 seconds, arrivals stay at 9 events per second and one worker completes 6 per second. Assume constant rates, no retries and no dropped events.

Try any step. Each has its own feedback.

1. How many events are pending after the burst?
A hint

Account for both the starting backlog and the net arrivals.

Compare the calculation

20 + (9 − 6) × 10 = 50 pending events. The worker continues processing during the burst.

2. Arrivals then fall to 3 per second. What happens to the backlog?

The worker has 6 − 3 = 3 events per second of spare capacity. The backlog shrinks; it cannot go below zero.

3. Connect the steps: when would a bigger buffer help?
Compare the whole problem

A larger buffer can hold the temporary excess if there is enough spare capacity afterwards to drain it. Sustained excess arrivals keep accumulating; storage alone does not raise processing capacity.

The missing receipt

A webhook worker asks an email service to send a message. The worker crashes before storing a local completion receipt.

The event is still pending locally. The email service may have accepted the request, but the worker did not retain its response.
What can the restarted worker conclude from the pending event alone?

A local pending marker can coexist with either remote outcome. The missing receipt is an information gap, not proof of failure or success.

Choose the next move and explain what evidence or contract it needs.
A hint

Separate the question “did it happen?” from “can repeating it be safe?”

Compare possible approaches
  • A durable remote receipt could settle the outcome if the service lets you look it up reliably.
  • A stable operation key can make retry safe when the service guarantees deduplication for that key and retry window.
  • Without either contract, describe the duplicate-send risk and how a human would resolve it.
